_Two outlets can report the same event and leave you with opposite impressions without either one stating a falsehood. Here is a fast, repeatable way to see the difference._

**Short answer:** pick one story, read three sources that disagree on emphasis, and compare four things: the headline, the first paragraph, who is quoted, and what is missing. It takes about five minutes and works on any topic.

## Why one source is never enough

Every outlet decides what to lead with, which experts to quote and which details to cut for space. Those choices shape what you take away, even when every sentence is accurate. Search engines add another layer: what you see first depends on ranking, personalization and your own earlier clicks. That is why comparing coverage on purpose beats trusting whatever lands on page one.

## The five-minute method

**1. Search for the event, not the opinion.** Use neutral words that describe what happened ("city council votes on zoning change"), not words that already carry a judgment. Loaded search terms return loaded results.

**2. Open three sources that are likely to differ.** For example: a wire service or local paper, an outlet that leans one way, and an outlet that leans the other. If you can find the primary document (the bill, the court filing, the study), open that too.

**3. Compare the headline and the first paragraph.** What is the story "about" in each one? A budget vote can be framed as a tax increase, a service cut or a routine adjustment. The framing is the first signal.

**4. Check who gets quoted.** List the voices in each piece. Is one side quoted at length and the other paraphrased in a single line? Are the experts named, and what is their stake?

**5. Look for what is missing.** The most useful question is the simplest: what does this version not tell me? Whatever appears in one source and is absent from the others is where to dig next.

## What to do with the differences

You do not need to decide which outlet is "right." Note where all three agree (that is likely the solid core of the story), where they disagree on facts (check the primary source), and where they disagree only on emphasis (that is framing, and you can now see it).

## FAQ

**How many sources should I compare?** Three is enough to spot framing differences without burning time. Add a primary document when one exists.

**Is a "neutral" source ever enough?** Wire services tend to be plainer, but they still choose what to include. Treat them as one input, not the answer.

**What if I do not know which outlets lean which way?** Start with the story itself. If three outlets frame it very differently, you have learned something regardless of their labels.

**Does this work for non-political news?** Yes. Health, technology and business stories get framed too, often by who funded the study or who benefits from the headline.
