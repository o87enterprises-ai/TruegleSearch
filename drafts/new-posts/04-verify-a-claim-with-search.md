_A screenshot, a statistic or a confident headline is not evidence by itself. This checklist turns a search engine into a fact-checking tool._

**Short answer:** find where the claim started, check whether independent sources confirm it, and test whether the evidence actually says what the claim says. Most false or misleading claims fail at one of those three steps.

## The checklist

**1. Write the claim as one plain sentence.** Vague claims cannot be checked. "Crime is up" becomes "violent crime in City X rose in 2026 compared with 2025." Now you know what evidence would settle it.

**2. Find the origin.** Search for distinctive words from the claim in quotation marks. Look for the earliest appearance. Many claims are repeated by hundreds of pages that all trace back to one post, one study or one quote with no source behind it.

**3. Go to the primary source.** If the claim cites a study, open the study. If it cites a statistic, find the dataset or the agency that publishes it. If it quotes a person, look for the full transcript or video. Secondary summaries often drop caveats.

**4. Check what the source actually says.** Compare the claim to the source: the same numbers, the same time period, the same population, the same conclusion. Misleading claims often use a real source for a different point.

**5. Look for independent confirmation.** Independent means separate reporting, not rewrites of the same press release. Search for the event from at least two outlets that do not share a common source.

**6. Look for credible disagreement.** Search the claim together with words like "disputed," "fact check" or "correction." Disagreement does not make a claim false, but you should know what the strongest objection is.

**7. Check the date and context.** Old images and quotes are often reshared as new. A reverse image search and a look at the original publication date catch a large share of recycled material.

**8. Ask who benefits.** Funding, affiliation and incentives do not prove something wrong, but they tell you where to look harder.

## Reading your results carefully

Search engines rank by relevance and popularity, not by truth. A claim repeated widely can outrank the correction. Scroll past the first screen, vary your wording and include the source type you want, such as a government database, a court record or a peer-reviewed paper.

## Where a multi-perspective search helps

When a claim is contested, viewing results through separate perspectives shows you how each camp presents the evidence, which makes it easier to find the strongest version of each argument. Truegle's perspective modes are designed for this, but the checklist works in any search tool.

## FAQ

**Is a fact-checking site enough?** It is a good starting point, but check its sources too. A fact-check is one more secondary source.

**What if I cannot find a primary source?** Treat the claim as unverified. Say so rather than repeating it.

**How do I verify a screenshot?** Search for the exact text, look for the original post or page, and check the date. Screenshots are easy to alter.

**How long should this take?** For a simple claim, five to ten minutes. If it takes longer, the claim is probably complicated or poorly sourced, and both are worth knowing.
